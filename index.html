What this actually is

A will says who gets what. It does not say where anything is.

What you leave sits across three systems that do not talk to each other, and nobody is in charge of joining them up. That job lands on the people you love, in the worst week of their lives.

The will

It names who inherits. It does not say where the accounts are, what the passwords are, or what you wanted. An executor can hold a perfectly valid will and still have no idea where to start.

Retirement accounts

A beneficiary designation can override your will, and an old 401(k) from an old job is among the most commonly lost things in an estate. Most people have never told anyone the account exists.

Accounts and the home

Banking, insurance, the mortgage, the utilities, the digital life. Every one held by a different company, every one with its own process, and not one of them will call your family first.

The plan inside it

It carries the whole path, in the order your family meets it.

Not a list of chapters. The actual sequence, from the first few hours through to closing the estate. The first stage of it is in the free Executor Toolkit, so you can read it before you buy anything.

STAGE 1

The first 48 hours

Secure · Notify · Record

Secure the home, the mail, the pets. Find the will, and find this book. The death certificate, then registering the death. Tell the employer about final pay, leave and retirement savings. Secure the valuables, and write down what they are.

STAGE 2

The first month

Notify · Claim · Protect

Apply for probate where it is needed. The banks, the retirement plans, Social Security, the insurers. Cancel or transfer the services. Claim the life insurance and the 401(k) or IRA death benefits. Identify the debts and keep the urgent bills paid.

STAGE 3

The first year

Settle · Account · Distribute

Letters Testamentary, then an estate bank account. Collect the assets, pay the debts in the order that applies. File the final tax return, deal with the property. Prepare the estate accounts and the distribution, then distribute and keep the records.

Rules and timeframes vary from state to state, and every estate is different. The Organizer points your executor to the right professional at each step rather than guessing on their behalf.

Questions

Good to know.

The things people ask before they decide.

I’ve already got a will. Do I still need this?

A will says who inherits. It does not tell anyone where your accounts are, what your passwords are, which retirement accounts exist, or what you wanted for your funeral. Your executor can be holding a perfectly valid will and still have no idea where to start. The Organizer sits beside the will and answers all of that.

Isn’t most of this free on the government websites?

Some of it is, and where a government service is the right answer we point you at it. What no government site does is put your accounts, your passwords, your policies, your wishes and your contacts into one document your family can pick up and follow. The information is scattered across a dozen agencies by design.

We’re not wealthy. Is this really for us?

It matters more, not less. A large estate usually has an attorney and an accountant already involved. A modest one is where a forgotten retirement account or a missing password does real damage, and where there is no professional standing by to sort it out.

Does it work in my state?

Yes. It’s about gathering your accounts, passwords, documents and wishes in one place, which matters everywhere. Where state-specific steps come up, it points you to the right professional rather than guessing for you.

Isn’t it a bit morbid?

It feels that way for about ten minutes. Then it feels like relief. The morbid version is the one where your family is guessing, ringing around, and opening drawers. Most readers tell us the odd thing is how calm they felt once it was done.

How long does it take to fill in?

A first pass in an afternoon gets the important things down. After that it is ten quiet minutes here and there. Nobody fills it in from cover to cover in one sitting, and it is not meant to be done that way.

It looks big. Do I really have to fill in all of it?

No. It is big because it covers a whole life, and you have had a lot of life. But not every section applies to everyone. If your children are grown, the dependants section is a page you skip. No property overseas, skip foreign assets. Each chapter has a “does not apply to me” tick, and the contents page keeps track. Some sections take a while, some take a minute, some you never open. What matters is that every section you could need is in there.

Is my information safe?

Yes. It’s a file you fill in on your own computer, or print and fill in by hand. Nothing is stored online or in anyone’s cloud, so there’s nothing to be breached. Where you keep it is entirely up to you.

Is this a subscription?

No. One payment, once. No account to log into, no renewal, no annual fee. Updates to the document are yours to keep.

Is this legal or financial advice?

No, and that’s by design. The Estate Organizer is a place to record your information and wishes. It isn’t a Will, and it doesn’t replace your attorney or accountant. It simply makes sure that when your family needs those professionals, they can find everything fast. Rules vary from state to state, so confirm the specifics with your own advisors.
