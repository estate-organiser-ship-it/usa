The plan

A will says who gets what. It doesn’t say what to do next.

The Organizer carries the whole path your executor walks, in the order they meet it. Every step below is already written out in the book, and the first stage is in the free Executor Toolkit.

STAGE 1

The first 48 hours

Secure · Notify · Record

  • Secure the home, the mail, the pets
  • Find the will, and find this book
  • The death certificate, and registering the death
  • Tell the employer: final pay, leave, retirement savings
  • Secure the valuables, and write down what they are

Without a planThe will found too late, the certificates ordered one at a time, valuables unrecorded.

STAGE 2

The first month

Notify · Claim · Protect

  • Apply for probate where it is required
  • Notify the banks, the retirement plans, Social Security, the insurers
  • Cancel or transfer the services
  • Claim the life insurance and the 401(k) or IRA death benefits
  • Identify the debts, keep the urgent bills paid

Without a planThe death re-explained on call after call, an old retirement account never claimed, an account frozen while the bills keep arriving.

STAGE 3

The first year

Settle · Account · Distribute

  • Letters Testamentary, then an estate bank account
  • Collect the assets, pay the debts in the order that applies
  • File the final tax return, deal with the property
  • Prepare the estate accounts and the distribution
  • Distribute, then keep the records

The gap

What you leave sits in three systems that don’t talk to each other.

No bank, no plan administrator and no government agency is in charge of joining them up. That job lands on the people you love, in the middle of their grief.

The will

It names who inherits. It does not tell anyone where the accounts are, what the passwords are, or what you wanted. Your executor can hold a valid will and still have no idea where to start.

In the Organizer: where the will is kept, who the executor is, and the message written to them.

Retirement accounts

A beneficiary designation can override your will, and old accounts from old jobs are among the most commonly lost assets in an estate. Most people have never told anyone the account even exists.

In the Organizer: every plan, every policy and the beneficiary details, in one list.

Accounts and the home

Accounts, insurance, the mortgage, the utilities, the digital life. Each one is held by a different company, each with its own process, and none of them will call your family first.

In the Organizer: every account, every login, how the home is held, and who to contact for each.

Questions

Good to know.

I’ve already got a will. Do I still need this?

A will says who inherits. It doesn’t tell anyone where your accounts are, what your passwords are, which retirement accounts exist, or what you wanted for your funeral. Your executor can be holding a perfectly valid will and still have no idea where to start. The Organizer is the document that sits beside the will and answers all of that. The two do different jobs.

Isn’t most of this free on the government websites?

Some of it is, and where a government service is the right answer the book points you at it. What no government site does is put your accounts, your passwords, your plans, your wishes and your contacts into one document your family can pick up and follow. The information is scattered across a dozen agencies by design.

We’re not wealthy. Is this really for us?

It matters more, not less. A large estate usually comes with an attorney and an accountant already involved. A modest one is the one where a forgotten retirement account, a joint account or a missing password does real damage, and where there is no professional standing by to sort it out.

Isn’t it a bit morbid?

Most people say the opposite once they start. It stops being about dying somewhere around the second page and becomes about tidying up: the accounts you forgot, the subscription still billing, the plan from a job two decades ago. Readers describe finishing it as a relief. It is a practical afternoon, not a sad one.

Is this another subscription that will bill me every year?

No. One payment, once. No account to log into, no renewal, no annual fee. Updates to the document are yours to keep.

How long does it take to fill in?

Most people work through it over a few weeks, not a weekend, one section at a time, when you've got the energy. There's no rush, and you can save and come back any time.

It looks big. Do I really have to fill in all of it?

No. It is big because it covers a whole life, and you have had a lot of life. But not every section applies to everyone. If your children are grown, the dependants section is a page you skip. No property overseas, skip foreign assets. Each chapter has a “does not apply to me” tick, and the contents page keeps track. Some sections take a while, some take a minute, some you never open. What matters is that every section you could need is in there.

Is my information safe?

Yes. It’s a file you fill in on your own computer, or print and fill in by hand. Nothing is stored online or in anyone’s cloud, so there’s nothing to be breached. Where you keep it is entirely up to you.

Is this legal or financial advice?

No, and that’s by design. The Estate Organizer is a place to record your information and wishes. It isn’t a Will, and it doesn’t replace your attorney or accountant. It simply makes sure that when your family needs those professionals, they can find everything fast. Rules vary from state to state, so confirm the specifics with your own advisors.

Does it work in my state?

Yes. It’s about gathering your accounts, passwords, documents and wishes in one place, which matters everywhere. Where state-specific steps come up, it points you to the right professional rather than guessing for you.
